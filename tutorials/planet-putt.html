A sky full of stars

Every game starts with an empty window. Ours is outer space, and space needs stars. Lots of them. Writing 90 lines of code, one per star, would be no fun, so we let a loop make them and keep them in a list.

  • const stars = [] makes an empty list. The for loop runs its block STAR_COUNT times, and each time stars.push adds one more star to the end of the list.
  • Each star is an object with an x, a y and a size. randomInt(0, WIDTH) picks a whole number somewhere across the screen.
  • randomChoice([1, 1, 1, 1.5, 2, 3]) picks one item from the list. There are three 1s, so most stars come out tiny, just like the real night sky.
  • In drawSky(), for (const star of stars) visits every star in the list, one at a time, and draws a little circle for it.
🔁
Quick refresher: lists and objects

A list (an array) keeps things in order: [5, 8, 13]. An object keeps things by name: { x: 5, y: 8 }. A list of objects is how games keep track of many things at once. Firefly Flash's swarm step covers this in depth.

The stars are made once, at the top of the file. What would happen if the star-making loop were inside draw() instead?

draw() runs about 60 times a second, so you'd get a brand-new random sky every frame, a fizzing mess, and the list would grow forever. Make things once; draw them every frame.
Think about how often draw() runs, and what randomInt does each time.

Velocity: how things move

Time for our hero: a little golf ball. It's an Actor, so it has a picture and a position. To make it move we give it a velocity: two numbers that say how fast it goes across and how fast it goes down.

  • ball.vx = 150 means "150 pixels to the right every second". ball.vy = -60 means 60 pixels up every second. On a screen, y grows downwards, so going up means a negative number.
  • Every frame, ball.x += ball.vx * dt nudges the ball a tiny bit. dt is the time since the last frame, about 1/60 of a second, so the ball moves 150 × 1/60 = 2.5 pixels each frame. Sixty little nudges add up to 150 pixels a second.
  • Click anywhere on the game to put the ball somewhere else. It keeps its velocity, so off it goes again.

Acceleration: a ball that falls

Our ball glides in a perfectly straight line, forever. Real thrown balls don't: they curve back down. That's because gravity keeps changing the ball's velocity, adding a little more "downwards" every moment. A change in velocity has a name: acceleration.

The only new idea is one line: ball.vy += FALL * dt. Every second, the downward speed grows by FALL. The ball starts going up fast (vy = -300), slows down, stops for an instant at the top, then falls faster and faster. Watch ball.vy climb in the live values. Click to throw it again.

Try 0: no acceleration, and you're back to a straight line. Try a negative number: the ball falls up!

In ball.vy += FALL * dt, what does FALL change directly?

Acceleration changes velocity, and then velocity changes position. Gravity never moves the ball directly. It only ever changes its speed.
Look at which variable is on the left of the +=.

Make the ball bounce off the bottom of the screen instead of falling out of sight. Add an if to update: when the ball is below the bottom and moving down, flip its vy around.

The bottom of the screen is HEIGHT. "Below the bottom" is ball.y > HEIGHT, and "moving down" is ball.vy > 0.

Flipping means making it negative: ball.vy = -ball.vy. Times 0.8 makes each bounce a bit smaller, like a real ball.

  if (ball.y > HEIGHT && ball.vy > 0) {
    ball.vy = -ball.vy * 0.8;
  }

Planet gravity

In space there's no "down". Instead, every planet pulls the ball towards itself. That's the same idea as FALL, an acceleration that changes the velocity, except now the direction and strength change depending on where the ball is. To work it out we need to answer three questions: which way, how far, and how hard.

1. Which way? An arrow from the ball to the planet

dx and dy are how far across and how far down the planet is from the ball. Together they make an arrow pointing straight at the planet.

2. How far? Pythagoras

The arrow's length is the distance. Picture a right-angled triangle: dx along the bottom, dy up the side, and the distance is the long slanted side. Pythagoras worked out, about 2,500 years ago, that distance = √(dx² + dy²), which in code is Math.sqrt(dx * dx + dy * dy).

3. How hard? Weaker with distance

pull = G × mass ÷ distance². Big planets pull harder, and the pull fades fast as you get further away. Twice as far means a quarter of the pull. That's how real gravity works too!

dx (across) dy (down) distance the pull

Then dx / distance shrinks the arrow to exactly one pixel long. That's a pure direction, which we multiply by pull and dt and add to the velocity, just like FALL did.

  • Math.max(…, planet.radius) never lets the distance be smaller than the planet. Right at a planet's centre the distance would be 0, and dividing by 0 would fling the ball to infinity!
  • body.vx -= body.vx * DRAG * dt is our space dust: every moment it takes away a slice of the speed, so shots slow down and stop, like a golf ball on grass.
  • moveBall(body, dt) works on any body with x, y, vx and vy, not just our ball. That will turn out to be a superpower in Step 9.
  • makePlanet bundles the numbers for the physics with an Actor for the picture.

Set DRAG to 0 and click near the planet: with no dust to slow it, the ball can loop around and around.

Quick refresher: mouse hooks

The game calls onMouseDown when you press, onMouseMove as you move, and onMouseUp when you let go. Each one gets pos, a list of two numbers: pos[0] is x and pos[1] is y. Taps on a phone count too. The scroll wheel counts as a mouse button, so onMouseDown ignores every button except mouse.LEFT; otherwise scrolling the page would start an aim!

  • When you press, we remember the spot in dragStart. While you drag, dragNow follows you. When you let go, the ball gets its new velocity.
  • dragStart − dragNow is the arrow from your finger back to where you pressed, which is the opposite of your pull. Pull left, it points right. Times POWER turns pixels of pull into speed.
  • If the speed is over MAX_SPEED, we shrink both parts by the same amount. Shrinking both keeps the direction exactly the same, only shorter.
  • drawAim draws the aiming arrow. The arrowhead uses Math.atan2, Math.cos and Math.sin to turn the arrow into an angle and back. You don't need to understand those yet: they're the standard recipe for "draw an arrowhead".

Strokes, stopping, and lost balls

This is starting to feel like golf. Real golf has rules, though: you can't hit the ball while it's still rolling, every hit counts as a stroke, and losing your ball costs you. Time to teach the game those rules.

  • state remembers what's happening: "aiming" or "flying". onMouseDown only starts aiming when the state is "aiming", so you can't hit a moving ball.
  • isResting is a tiny timer. While the ball is very slow, stillTime counts up. Speed up again and it's back to 0. Slow for 0.3 seconds? The ball has stopped. (Watch ball.stillTime in the live values at the end of a shot.)
  • lostInSpace asks four questions joined by ||, which means "or". Off the left, or off the right, or off the top, or off the bottom.
  • loseBall adds a penalty stroke and puts the ball back at shotFrom, the spot where you hit it. showMessage shows a message for 2 seconds.
  • write() is a little helper for putting words on screen. anchor || "midTop" means "use the anchor you were given, or if there isn't one, use midTop".

Why does onMouseDown check if (state === "aiming")?

Exactly. Without it, you could keep whacking the ball in mid-air. A state variable is how a game knows which rules apply right now.
Try removing the check (or setting state back to "aiming" yourself) and see what the player could do mid-flight.

Put the stroke count on the screen, like Strokes: 2 in the top-right corner.

Bouncing off planets

Let's make planets solid. When the ball touches one, two things happen: we push the ball back out to the surface, and we change its velocity so it bounces. The bounce is the clever bit, and it all hangs on one special arrow.

The normal: "straight out of the surface"

The arrow from the planet's centre to the ball, shrunk to 1 pixel long, points straight out of the planet's surface. Game makers call it the normal: nx and ny.

outward = vx × nx + vy × ny measures how much of the velocity points along the normal, out of the planet. If it's negative, the ball is heading into the planet. Then we split the velocity into two pieces: the part sliding along the surface, and the part going in. The going-in part flips around (shrunk by BOUNCE); the sliding part carries on (shrunk a little by GRIP).

  • distance >= touching skips planets the ball isn't touching. They touch when the distance between centres is less than the two radii added together.
  • bonk squishes the planet a little, then animate(…, tween: "spring") wobbles it back to its normal size. Cute and squishy!
  • Now moveBall returns the planet it bonked (or null, meaning "none"), so update can react.

What happens if you set BOUNCE to 0?

The "going in" part gets multiplied by 0, so nothing pushes it back out, but the sliding part still carries it along. Try it with the slider!

Into the wormhole

Every golf course needs a hole. Ours is a swirling wormhole. Getting the ball inside it is just a distance check (Pythagoras again!), and then a little celebration animation.

  • inWormhole is true when the ball's centre is closer to the wormhole's centre than HOLE_RADIUS.
  • sinkBall uses animate to change five things at once: slide the ball to the middle, shrink it to almost nothing, and spin it two whole turns (720°), in 0.9 seconds.
  • onFinished: finishHole hands animate a function to call later, when the animation ends. Notice there are no brackets: we're passing the function itself, not calling it now.
  • wormhole.angle -= 120 * dt spins the wormhole 120 degrees every second.
  • The ball's ouch face from the last challenge is in here too. clock.scheduleUnique(resetFace, 0.4) puts the normal face back 0.4 seconds later.

When does finishHole run?

animate holds on to the function and calls it when it's done. That's called a callback: "call me back when you're finished".
Read onFinished out loud: "on finished, do…".

Make the wormhole spin the other way, and faster: at least 200 degrees a second, clockwise on the screen.

The spin comes from wormhole.angle -= 120 * dt in update. -= makes the angle go down.

Change -= to += to turn the other way, and 120 to a bigger number.

Seeing the future

Here's a magic trick: a dotted line that shows where the ball will go, curves and bounces and all, before you've even hit it. How can the game know the future? Easy: it plays the future, really fast, with a pretend ball.

  • predictPath makes a ghost object with the ball's position and the velocity you're aiming with. Then a loop calls the very same moveBall on it 180 times. That's 1.5 seconds of pretend flight, worked out in a blink.
  • Every 6th step it drops a dot (i % 6 === 0 means "i divides by 6 exactly").
  • This is the payoff from Step 4: because moveBall works on any body, the ghost follows exactly the same physics as the real ball.

Why the ball now moves in equal steps

Frames don't all take the same time: one might be 1/60 of a second, the next a bit longer. If the real ball moved by whatever dt happened to be, its path would drift slightly away from the prediction. So the real ball now moves in equal steps of STEP (1/120 of a second), just like the ghost.

leftover works like a piggy bank of time. Each frame drops dt in; the while loop spends it in STEP-sized coins, one physics step per coin, and keeps the change for next frame. Same steps, same path: the prediction comes true every time.

Try 6 seconds. Now the game is too easy! Picking numbers like this, enough to help but not enough to spoil the fun, is a big part of game design.

Why do the real ball and the ghost both move in steps of exactly STEP?

Physics done in different-sized steps lands in slightly different places. Same steps in, same path out.
Think about what would happen to the dotted line if the real ball moved differently from the ghost.

Pack the dots closer together: drop a dot every 3 steps instead of every 6. Then aim a shot to see it.

Find i % 6 === 0 in predictPath.

    if (i % 3 === 0) dots.push([ghost.x, ghost.y]);

Six holes and a scorecard

One hole is a toy; six holes is a game. Instead of writing code for each hole, we describe every hole as data: a list of objects saying where the tee, the wormhole and the planets are. One function, startHole, can set up any of them.

  • HOLES is a list of objects, and each object has its own list of planets. HOLES[0] is the first hole (lists count from 0), and HOLES[0].planets[0].kind is "pink".
  • startHole(index) reads one hole's data, makes its planets with makePlanet, and moves the wormhole, flag and ball into place.
  • scores.push(strokes) remembers each hole's strokes. nextHole goes on to the next one, or to the scorecard after the last.
  • SCORE_NAMES turns "strokes minus par" into golf words: one under par is a birdie, two under is an eagle.
  • storage.set saves your best total so it's still there next time, and storage.get reads it back when the game starts. Press R to start a hole over.

What is HOLES[2].planets[0].kind?

HOLES[2] is the third hole, "Twin Moons" (counting 0, 1, 2), and its first planet is the green one.
Lists count from 0, so HOLES[2] is the third hole in the list. Look for "Twin Moons" in the code.

Polish: sound, sparkle, and a title screen

The game works! The last step is the sprinkles on top that make it feel finished. This is the complete game, exactly the one you played at the top of the page.

  • Sounds. tone.play makes a beep with no sound file at all: a note (a name like "C3" or a number of Hz) and how many seconds to play it. playNotes plays a little tune by scheduling each note a bit later than the last.
  • Twinkling. Math.sin makes a number that swings smoothly up and down forever as time grows, like a wave. Each star gets its own twinkle head start, so they don't all blink together.
  • Sparkles and clouds. drawSparkle draws a 4-pointed star from 8 points, and the soft clouds are see-through circles stacked on top of each other.
  • Standing tall. standUp uses angleTo to stand the ball upright on whatever planet it lands on, like a tiny astronaut.
  • A title screen is just one more state, "title". Rounded panels come from roundBox: two rectangles in a plus shape with a circle on each corner.
  • A rounded font. Adding fontname: "fredoka" to write() changes every word in the game at once. That's the payoff of a helper function!

Give the bonk your own sound. Change the note or waveform of the tone.play in bonk, then bonk a planet to hear it.

Waveforms are "sine", "square", "triangle" and "sawtooth". Notes go from low like "C2" to high like "C6".

Words you learned

Velocity
How fast something moves and which way, as two numbers: vx across and vy down.
Acceleration
How fast the velocity changes. Gravity is an acceleration.
dt
Seconds since the last frame. Multiply speeds by it so games run the same everywhere.
Pythagoras
distance = √(dx² + dy²): the length of the slanted side of a right-angled triangle.
Inverse square
A pull that divides by distance × distance: twice as far, a quarter as strong.
Direction (unit vector)
An arrow exactly 1 long. Divide dx and dy by the distance to get one.
Normal
An arrow pointing straight out of a surface. Bounces flip the part of the velocity along it.
Drag
A force that slows things down a bit every moment, like our space dust.
Fixed time step
Doing physics in equal-sized steps, so the same shot always takes the same path.
Simulation
Running pretend physics ahead of time, like the prediction line's ghost ball.
Callback
A function you hand over to be called later, like onFinished.
Data-driven levels
Describing levels as lists and objects, so one function can build them all.
